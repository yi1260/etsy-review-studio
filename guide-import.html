Importing Etsy reviews into Shopify or Judge.me: what's free, what's legal, what breaks

Moving an Etsy shop to Shopify is the easy half. The reviews are where people get stuck — not because the data is hard to move, but because there are three different routes with different coverage, and one of them is illegal if you do it wrong.

This is the practical version: which route to try first, what each one actually carries over, and where the FTC line sits.

Route 1 — Judge.me's free Etsy sync (try this first if you use Judge.me)

If Judge.me is your review app, they ship a native Etsy import on the free plan. You connect the shop, it pulls reviews over, and it keeps syncing on a schedule.

This is the right answer for most people, and it costs nothing. Two things to check before you rely on it:

  • Coverage. A sync typically covers your active listings with written reviews. Rating-only reviews, reviews on deactivated or deleted listings, and reviews on listings you've since restructured often fall outside it.
  • Portability. A sync puts the reviews inside Judge.me. It doesn't hand you a file. If you later switch review apps, or want an offline archive, you're back to square one.

So: free, fast, and good — as long as your shop is simple and you're staying on Judge.me.

Route 2 — Etsy's own review download (free, undocumented fields)

Etsy does let sellers download their reviews. It's buried:

Shop Manager → Settings → Options → Download Data
  → Take your data with you → Download Your Reviews

Most sellers don't know it exists, which is why "Etsy won't give you your reviews" is such a common (and wrong) claim online.

The catch is that Etsy's help article doesn't publish the column list. You get a file; you don't get a schema. So open it in a spreadsheet before you build anything on top of it, and verify four things:

  1. The review body and date — the obvious ones.
  2. Your seller replies — these are conversational context, and they're usually the first thing lost in a migration.
  3. The original full-resolution buyer photo URLs — not thumbnails. Thumbnail URLs are usually resized derivatives and look bad on a storefront.
  4. The listing each review belongs to — a listing ID or URL you can map to a Shopify product handle.

If all four are there, you may not need any tool. If one is missing, you need a different source for that column, because you cannot reconstruct a seller reply or an original photo URL after the fact.

Route 3 — a structured CSV you generate yourself

The manual version: collect rating, date, reviewer name, body, listing URL and photo URLs into a spreadsheet, then import.

The reason people build tools for this is that the last two columns are the annoying ones — photo URLs are often only present in the page's rendered markup, and listing URLs need normalising to something you can join on.

However you get there, the import is the same shape: one row per review, joined to a product on a stable key.

review_date, rating, reviewer_name, body, picture_urls, product_handle, seller_reply
2026-03-14,  5,      @someone,      "Arrived...", https://i.etsystatic.com/.../il_fullxfull.123.jpg, silk-scarf-no-7, "Thank you!"

Map product_handle (or product_id) to your Shopify handle, not the product title. Titles get edited; handles are what the storefront resolves. If you match on title, a rename silently drops the review.

Where the legal line is

This part gets skipped in most tutorials, and it's the part that can actually cost you.

The FTC's Rule on Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (16 CFR Part 465, in force since October 2024) targets fake and deceptive reviews. Two provisions matter when you migrate:

  • Reviews have to be for the same product. You can bring your own reviews with you when you move the same product to a new storefront. You cannot take reviews written about product A and display them on product B — even if you made both, even if they're similar.
  • Reviews have to come from your own customers. Displaying another seller's reviews as if they were yours is a misrepresentation, whether you scraped them, imported them, or a supplier handed them to you.

The practical rule for a migration: import reviews product-by-product, from your own shop, and don't merge variants that are meaningfully different products. If you consolidated five Etsy listings into one Shopify product, that's usually fine (same items, different packaging). If you folded a sticker listing's reviews into a mug listing because "it's all my shop", that's the fact pattern the rule is aimed at.

Two more things people get wrong:

  • Don't filter to only 5-star reviews. Selective display that misrepresents the overall rating is a separate problem from fake reviews.
  • Public review data is for research, not for your storefront. Ranking a competitor's bestsellers by review volume is legitimate market research. Copying their buyers' words onto your product page is not.

A short checklist

  1. Using Judge.me? Try the free Etsy sync first — then check whether rating-only and inactive-listing reviews came across.
  2. Want a file you own? Use Etsy's official download, and inspect the columns before planning around them.
  3. Either way, verify seller replies, original photo URLs and listing identifiers survived.
  4. Import per product, join on handle, keep your own reviews only.
  5. Keep an offline copy. Review history is the one asset you can't re-earn retroactively.
